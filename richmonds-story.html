Change Isn't New to Richmond

Change isn't new to Richmond, and more change is coming.

The two timelines below tell that story: one shows how Richmond's economy has always changed over time, and the other highlights recent plans, projects, and milestones building toward a just transition now.

Richmond has been shaped by major economic transitions for thousands of years, from Ohlone economies rooted in land, water, and trade to colonization, ranching, railroads, oil refining, wartime shipbuilding, suburbanization, disinvestment, and service-sector work. Each transition transformed the city, often bringing opportunity for some while deepening harm for others through land theft, racial exclusion, pollution, displacement, and economic instability.

Read more

Today, Richmond faces another defining transition: moving beyond fossil fuel dependence toward a just, sustainable, and community-centered economy. This timeline connects Richmond's past economic shifts with recent transition milestones, plans, and projects that are helping shape the city's future.

Richmond's economy has never stood still. For thousands of years, Ohlone people sustained local economies through harvesting, fishing, hunting, and trade before colonization violently disrupted Indigenous life, land, and culture. Since then, Richmond has experienced repeated economic transformations: from colonial ranching to railroads, refineries, agriculture, explosives, and wine production; from the World War II shipbuilding boom to postwar disinvestment, suburbanization, and the rise of low-wage service work.

These transitions brought growth and opportunity for some, while also causing deep harm through land theft, racial exclusion, incarceration, pollution, displacement, and economic abandonment. Today, Richmond is entering another major transition: moving away from dependence on fossil fuels and toward a more just, sustainable, and community-rooted economy. This timeline traces Richmond's past economic shifts alongside recent just-transition milestones, plans, and projects that point toward a healthier and more equitable future.

1 — A Long History of Change

Changing Economies

Richmond has been an Ohlone shoreline, mission territory, a railroad and refinery town, a wartime boomtown, and a city left to live with the consequences of each transformation — long before "just transition" had a name.

Pre-1770s

Ohlone Economy

For thousands of years, Ohlone people cared for the shoreline, marshes, and hills that became Richmond — fishing, gathering, making goods, and trading throughout the Bay Area.

Read more

People have been living in and around Richmond, and therefore have had a local economy, going back at least 5,000 years. Ohlone people harvested "an astounding variety of seeds, nuts, fruits, and bulbs" and hunted and fished. They had extensive trade networks through which they obtained obsidian, bows, tobacco, basketry materials, and pigments for painting. But the intentional erasure of Ohlone language, culture, and ways of organizing society makes knowledge of their economies extremely limited — and also brought about the earliest known economic transition.

Land stolen, Ohlone economies and ways of life disrupted

1770s–1850s

Colonization & Spanish Missions

Spanish colonization and the mission system forcibly displaced Ohlone people from their land and labor, breaking existing economies and land stewardship.

Read more

The transition from a long-standing subsistence economy in Richmond started the day the Ohlone people first met colonizing Europeans, on March 28th, 1772. A group of 14 Spanish soldiers on horseback arrived at a small town on San Pablo Creek. According to the journal written by the Spanish record-keeper, a group of Ohlone people were processing Brodieia bulbs and soaproot to eat. Over the next six decades, the Spanish established missions and attempted to replace Ohlone culture with their own through violence, assimilation, religious indoctrination, and kidnapping and enslaving children. An Ohlone woman named Maria Colos was interviewed around 1904 and described a past kidnapping: "a wagon filled with Indian children coming from Martinez… they were bringing them like animals… They wanted some water to drink. They were naked." In 1883, ownership of Richmond was given by the Mexican government to Francisco Castro, a Spanish soldier stationed in California.

Mission economy collapses, industry rushes in

1900s–1910s

Industrial Founding: Railroads & Refinery

The Santa Fe Railway's most western stop and Standard Oil's 1902 refinery turn Richmond into an industrial town almost overnight.

Read more

A second economic transition came about when Richmond's economy shifted away from colonial ranches to settler industries. Chinese and other workers laid the final tracks of the transcontinental railroad in Point Richmond in 1869. The large marsh on the eastern side of Point Richmond was filled in, and the Standard Oil refinery was established in 1901. What would become the largest winery in California at the time, Winehaven, was built. The largest dynamite manufacturer in the US, the Giant Powder Company, moved its operations to what is now Point Pinole Park. Richmond also had thriving agricultural businesses in the early 1900s and became a hub for flower cultivation. Richmond officially incorporated as a city in 1905.

Wartime demand quadruples the population overnight through the Great Migration

1940s

WWII Shipbuilding Boom

Kaiser Shipyards bring a wartime population boom, recruiting waves of Black workers from across the country to build ships around the clock.

Read more

In the 1940s, Richmond went through its most famous economic transition — the shipbuilding boom facilitated by federal spending for World War II. The Kaiser Shipbuilding Corporation had an out-of-state recruitment program that aimed to secure 150 new workers per week for its yards in Richmond. In the decade of the 1940s, the city's population quadrupled from 23,000 to nearly 100,000. This included the arrival of African Americans to Richmond, who, according to the 1940 US Census, numbered 270, and by 1950, were 13,374. African Americans brought traditions that shaped the local economy, including midwifery, a thriving music scene, social clubs, sports, and crops and foods from the South.

Racial violence, redlining, discrimination by realtors and banks, and other forms of racial exclusion and dispossession controlled the access of African Americans and other people of color to work, homes, land, financing, and other essentials for economic well-being. Japanese Americans in Richmond, who had built up much of the flower-growing industry, were rounded up by the federal government and incarcerated in internment camps for several years. Many came home to their properties having been taken or damaged.

Jobs vanish as shipyards close and suburbs draw residents out of the city

1950s–1970s

Post-Wartime Decline & Suburbanization

Shipyards close, jobs and white residents move to new suburbs, and Richmond's tax base and job center hollow out.

Read more

The economic transition following World War II was one of economic decline and abandonment, as federal funding for shipbuilding was withdrawn, manufacturers moved out of Richmond to whites-only areas like Milpitas, and many white Richmond residents took advantage of subsidized suburban development and moved out of the city. In 1950, Richmond had 80,000 white residents, and within a decade that number had fallen to 56,000, while the African American population had increased slightly.

The new economy was one of suburbanization — where public infrastructure and business development prioritized automobile use and the needs of commuters passing through Richmond. The 580 freeway connecting I-80 to the Richmond-San Rafael Bridge was built, as was the Richmond Parkway. Hilltop Mall was developed, and many of the businesses in downtown Richmond moved to the mall or closed. This economic transition replaced higher-wage industrial jobs with low-wage retail and service jobs. Tax policy changes made by Proposition 13 in 1978, and other policies, gutted local public funds, undermining city services and public schools.

Disinvestment turns into policing and prisons

1980s–1990s

Mass Incarceration Era

Disinvestment, over-policing, and the war on drugs drive decades of incarceration concentrated in Richmond's Black and Latino neighborhoods.

Read more

The impact of mass incarceration and the influx of drugs and guns in the 1980s and 1990s had a devastating effect on Richmond's local economy. The racist effect of mass incarceration resulted in more African American adults in prison, jail, or on probation or parole than were enslaved in 1850, a decade before the Civil War began, according to Michelle Alexander. The incarceration of Black men was so extreme that by 2002, there were "nearly 3 million more black adult women than men in black communities across the United States, a gender gap of 26 percent." The economic impact of this trauma and lost time included losses in formal education, career development, growth of businesses, social networks, mentorship, and other key factors for economic well-being.

Organizing on our own terms to plan the next transition

2000s–Present

Towards a Just Transition

Richmond organizes — through policy, campaigns, coalition, and planning — to shape what comes after the refinery on its own terms.

Read more

In the last 20 years, Richmond has embarked on another economic transition: one focused on a more just and sustainable economy. The city has stood out nationally in its efforts to train people to access green jobs, prevent foreclosures, protect tenants, end over-policing, and support people returning from incarceration, ensure economic development has equitable benefits, and shift toward renewable energy. This transition is still in progress, but its champions see the present as a pivotal moment to scale up progress.

That history leads directly into what's happening right now — the plans, projects, and milestones building toward Richmond's just transition today.
