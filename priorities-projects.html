14 Priority Projects

Community workshops and existing city and regional plans identified 14 priority projects that could help build a just, thriving, and fossil-fuel-free Richmond economy.

These projects are practical building blocks for the future residents have described: good jobs, community ownership, healthy neighborhoods, affordable housing, clean energy, and opportunities for people who have historically been excluded.

Project plans will continue to change as community needs, partnerships, funding, and conditions evolve. For the latest information about a project's status and funding needs, follow its "Current Status & Funding" link to its profile on Blueprint.

How were these projects chosen?

Setting priorities for Richmond's future economy means bringing together four essential elements:

  • What residents and workers need now
  • Residents' dreams and aspirations for Richmond's future economy
  • What capacity already exists to plan and support projects
  • What additional resources, partnerships, and expertise are needed to carry them out

No single group holds all of this knowledge, expertise, and lived experience. Residents most affected by pollution, displacement, discrimination, low wages, and other failures of the current economy understand the community's needs best. Many Richmond adults and elders also carry important knowledge about previous eras in the city's history, including city programs and initiatives that did respond to residents' needs.

Planning projects is only one part of transforming Richmond's economy. Carrying them out requires people who understand the systems that make projects possible — planners in government, community organizations, and businesses who bring knowledge of policies, funding, governance, land use, and coordination, alongside workers, business owners, researchers, and project partners who bring practical knowledge about what it takes to launch, operate, and sustain the work.

That is why Our Future Economy combines research, community planning, and creative activities to identify priorities. We also asked a larger question: how do we plan for an economy we have never experienced? Imagining a Richmond that is free from fossil fuels and supports the well-being of all workers and residents requires more than solving today's problems — it requires radical imagination, the ability to picture a future beyond the limits of the economy we know now.

Through visualization, participatory theater, community discussion, and examples from other places, residents explored what that future could look like and what projects could help build it. Projects that matched the priorities raised in workshops were brought back to residents for discussion, feedback, and prioritization. The result is a set of projects that connects community visions with existing plans and real opportunities for action.

A community-strengthening process: the projects presented here are not owned by Our Future Economy. Most were first imagined, planned, or advanced by other community leaders, organizations, agencies, and residents. Our role is to help bring information together, strengthen community participation, connect related efforts, and build greater support for projects that can benefit Richmond residents. This list is a snapshot of the ideas and priorities that emerged through the planning process — the projects will continue to evolve as plans are refined and community needs change.

Ownership & Business

Cooperative Grocery Stores

What Is the Project?

Community-owned grocery stores are opening in Richmond neighborhoods that need better access to healthy food. These stores sell fresh, affordable, and culturally familiar foods while creating good local jobs.

Workers, shoppers, or both own the stores and help make important decisions. Cooperation Richmond is developing a 3,100-square-foot cooperative grocery store in North Richmond, and residents have called for more stores in other parts of Richmond.

How Does It Help the Community?
Community ownership
Workers and shoppers help own the store and decide how it operates. This keeps more money and decision-making power in the community.
Good local jobs
Workers earn fair wages, share in the store's success, and build ownership. A six-month paid training program helps people prepare to become worker-owners.
Fresh, affordable food
Residents can buy affordable, fresh produce and foods that reflect Richmond's many cultures — including from Urban Tilth's North Richmond Farm.
Cleaner air
The store could install strong air filters to provide a safer indoor space during wildfire smoke and periods of heavy pollution.
Less travel for groceries
A neighborhood grocery store saves residents time and transportation costs. It also reduces driving, air pollution, and fossil-fuel use.
What's Happening Now?

Project partners are planning the store, studying what local shoppers need, and designing how community ownership will work. They are also hiring an operations manager, securing the store site, raising money for construction and equipment, preparing a paid worker-owner training program, and planning the store's food and product selection.

Who's Working On It?
Cooperation RichmondProject lead
Urban TilthLocal produce partner
Mandela Grocery CooperativeCooperative training partner
Community Housing Development Corp.Construction & development
Safe Return ProjectCommunity accountability
Richmond LANDCommunity accountability
What's Still Needed?

More funding to cover construction, equipment, food inventory, training, and fair wages. Partners also need to complete agreements, strengthen coordination, and purchase refrigeration and clean-air equipment.

How Can You Help?

Spread the word about the store and upcoming fundraising efforts. Residents can also ask local and regional leaders to provide public funding for the project.

Cooperative Business Incubator & Loan Program

What Is the Project?

The Cooperative Business Incubator helps Richmond residents start worker-owned businesses. It gives entrepreneurs training and support with business plans, legal paperwork, finances, and daily operations.

Cooperation Richmond plans to work with five Richmond entrepreneurs or small businesses that want to become cooperatives. A related loan program could offer grants and low-interest loans, and could also create ways for Richmond residents to invest in local businesses.

How Does It Help the Community?
Worker ownership
Workers help own the business, share in profits, and vote on major decisions.
Good jobs and shared wealth
Residents can move from unstable or low-paid work into ownership and build long-term wealth.
Money stays in Richmond
Locally owned businesses keep more money moving through Richmond instead of sending profits to large companies outside the city.
Fair and safe workplaces
Workers have a direct say in wages, safety, schedules, and how the business operates.
What's Happening Now?

Project partners are recruiting the first group of five entrepreneurs and creating the training program. The current plan includes basic cooperative training, help with business plans and legal registration, and access to funding for new business launches.

About $200,000 for training, legal costs, technical help, and payments for participants. The project also needs mentors, a final focus for the first group of businesses, and a clear application process.

How Can You Help?

Help identify Richmond entrepreneurs and small-business owners who may want to join the program. Residents can also support policies that give local worker-owned businesses a better chance to win city contracts.

Housing

Restoration of Abandoned Housing

What Is the Project?

This project repairs abandoned public and private homes across Richmond and makes them permanently affordable. Future residents, including people who have experienced homelessness or incarceration, could help renovate the homes and earn credit toward ownership. The project would give priority to people who were displaced from the property or from Richmond.

Richmond LAND is planning to restore 12 homes at the former Las Deltas public housing site in North Richmond. The Richmond Community Foundation also runs a program that repairs abandoned homes for moderate-income, first-time Richmond homebuyers.

How Does It Help the Community?
Stable, affordable housing
Abandoned homes become safe and affordable places for Richmond residents to live.
Right to return
People who were displaced from Richmond or lost their homes here receive priority for the restored housing.
Construction training
Residents gain paid construction experience and can earn certificates that help them enter union apprenticeships.
Good careers
Trainees can move into construction jobs that offer family-supporting wages, benefits, and retirement plans.
Safer neighborhoods
Repairing empty and damaged homes reduces hazards and brings life back to neighborhood blocks.
What's Happening Now?

Richmond LAND controls the North Richmond properties and is raising money to begin environmental cleanup, construction training, and early renovation work. The Richmond Housing Renovation Program has already repaired 26 homes.

Who's Working On It?
Cooperation RichmondFundraising & workforce coordination
Richmond LANDCommunity land trust & developer
Cypress Mandela Training CenterConstruction training
Richmond Community FoundationHousing Renovation Program
SparkPointHomebuyer training & support
What's Still Needed?

The North Richmond project needs about $300,000 for worker payments, instructors, equipment, and early construction work. The homes may also need lead, asbestos, and other environmental cleanup before renovation can begin. Partners need stronger agreements with building trade unions so trainees can move directly into apprenticeships.

How Can You Help?

Support funding for abandoned-home restoration and paid construction training. Community groups can also help recruit young people, returning residents, and others who face barriers to construction careers.

Construction of Social Housing

What Is the Project?

Social housing provides permanently affordable apartments for people with low and moderate incomes. Rent is based on what residents can afford — a person does not lose their home just because their income increases.

The housing can serve families, elders, people with disabilities, teachers, nonprofit workers, survivors of abuse, former foster youth, people returning from incarceration, and residents who have experienced homelessness, and can connect residents with case managers, life-skills programs, health support, and help during unemployment.

How Does It Help the Community?
Permanently affordable homes
The housing remains affordable over time instead of becoming market-rate housing.
Housing for working people
Social housing can serve workers who earn too much for some housing programs but cannot afford Bay Area rents.
Protection from displacement
Residents can stay in their homes as their income changes.
Support during hard times
Partnerships with service providers can help residents facing health problems, unemployment, or other major life changes.
What's Happening Now?

California is developing a statewide social housing program that may create new funding and support. The West Contra Costa Unified School District is studying possible uses for extra public land, including housing for teachers and other workers. City and community leaders are also discussing a social housing pilot in Richmond.

Who's Working On It?
Councilmember Claudia JimenezRichmond city leadership
Zach LouCalifornia Green New Deal Coalition
Terner Center at UC BerkeleyResearch & planning
Tenants TogetherTenant advocacy
Liberation in a GenerationEconomic justice partner
What's Still Needed?

Richmond needs funding for a local pilot project because state and federal funding is not yet enough. The project also needs public land owned by the city, school district, or another public agency.

How Can You Help?

Support the use of public land for permanently affordable housing. Residents can also ask city and state leaders to fund a Richmond social housing pilot.

Green Jobs & Industry

Battery Manufacturing

What Is the Project?

Battery manufacturing businesses make batteries for electric cars, e-bikes, appliances, energy storage, and other clean technologies. Richmond could use its industrial land, port, and skilled workforce to grow this industry, with local hiring agreements and job-training programs helping residents qualify for the new jobs.

Strong safety rules would protect workers, nearby neighborhoods, the air, and the water from pollution and harmful waste.

How Does It Help the Community?
Good green jobs
Battery manufacturing could create hundreds of living-wage jobs with benefits.
A new local industry
The project could help Richmond build an economy that is less dependent on oil and other fossil fuels.
Training and career paths
Local training programs could prepare residents for skilled manufacturing jobs.
Health and safety protections
Strong rules could prevent pollution, unsafe working conditions, and harmful waste disposal.
What's Happening Now?

The UC Berkeley Labor Center is studying how battery manufacturing could grow and create good union jobs in Contra Costa County. The Green Empowerment Zone may also help support this industry in West Contra Costa County. Bay Area Jobs First selected BAM Consulting to lead a regional battery manufacturing plan and build partnerships across the industry.

Who's Working On It?
UC Berkeley Labor CenterIndustry & jobs research
City of Richmond Economic Development Dept.Local economic development
Green Empowerment ZoneRegional support
BAM ConsultingRegional planning
What's Still Needed?

Clear local leadership, more funding, and stronger agreements about the roles of the city, employers, and workforce programs. More information is also needed about the types of jobs, required training, safety protections, and investments Richmond should make.

How Can You Help?

Residents can support strong local-hiring, union, health, and environmental standards for any battery business that comes to Richmond.

All-Electric Building Retrofits

What Is the Project?

Local workers replace gas water heaters, furnaces, stoves, and other appliances with electric models. They also add insulation and make other improvements that help homes use less energy — helping Richmond homes move away from fossil fuels, improve indoor air, and lower energy use.

Strong protections are needed so low-income residents do not face higher energy bills after the upgrades.

How Does It Help the Community?
Cleaner indoor air
Removing gas-burning appliances reduces indoor pollution.
Lower energy use
Insulation and efficient electric appliances help homes waste less energy.
Good local jobs
The work creates jobs for electricians, plumbers, heating and cooling technicians, construction workers, project managers, engineers, and permit staff.
A cleaner economy
The upgrades reduce fossil-fuel use and support Richmond's climate goals.
What's Happening Now?

Richmond BUILD provides training for residents who want to enter related construction and energy careers. Richmond Rising runs a smaller home-upgrade program in the Iron Triangle, Santa Fe, and Coronado neighborhoods. The current program serves about 10 people each year and could be expanded.

More funding is needed to serve additional households and neighborhoods. The program also needs protections that keep electricity costs affordable for low-income residents.

Creative Reuse & Maker Space

What Is the Project?

A creative reuse and maker space collects wood, furniture, tools, and other useful materials before they are sent to the landfill. Residents use the materials for woodworking, repairs, art, furniture, and new products.

The space also offers business planning, mentorship, and skills classes for formerly incarcerated residents and others who want to start a small business.

How Does It Help the Community?
Jobs and small businesses
The project could create work and business opportunities for five to 10 residents.
Support for returning residents
People coming home from incarceration gain training, mentorship, and a place to build new skills.
Less waste
Reusable materials stay out of the landfill and become useful products.
Cleaner air and climate
Reducing waste and making products from reused materials can lower pollution and greenhouse gas emissions.
What's Happening Now?

The project is still an idea and does not yet have a full plan or lead organization. RecycleMore has started Fix-it Clinics where residents can learn to repair and reuse items — these clinics could help test parts of the project.

Youth career training

Inclusive care
Trained staff support children with disabilities and different learning or sensory needs.

Black Wellness Center

What Is the Project?

The Black Wellness Center is a safe, Black-led place where Black residents and their loved ones come together for support, healing, and community. The center focuses on mental, physical, and emotional health, and also provides education and support related to money, housing, work, justice, and other issues that affect well-being.

The center connects residents with trusted services and supports people displaced from Richmond, people returning from incarceration, elders, and residents who need help paying for medicine.

How Does It Help the Community?
Black-led services
Black residents help decide what services are needed and how the center operates.
Mental, physical, and emotional health
Residents receive care and support that responds to their full well-being.
Community trust and accountability
Clear public information shows where resources go and how residents can receive services.
Support for elders and returning residents
The center provides focused help for seniors, people coming home from incarceration, and people displaced from Richmond.
Paid community leadership
Community members can receive payment for advisory roles that guide the center and keep it accountable.
What's Happening Now?

Contra Costa County awarded $1 million to 13 Black-led organizations for mobile and pop-up wellness services in East and West County. The next step is to build lasting funding and identify Black-led organizations with the capacity to lead a larger, permanent project. Partners also need to study Black residents' past and current experiences with health and social services.

Who's Working On It?

Long-term county and local funding. It also needs a strong Black-led organization or group of organizations to lead the center, assess community needs, and coordinate services.

How Can You Help?

Paid youth jobs
Young adults can work as Community Ambassadors and gain experience with farmers markets, cooking, farming, and community outreach.

Transportation

Free Public Transportation

What Is the Project?

Richmond creates a no-fee transportation zone through a partnership with AC Transit or an expansion of Richmond Moves. Residents ride buses or local shuttles without paying. Free service makes it easier for people with low incomes to reach jobs, schools, stores, health care, and other important places.

How Does It Help the Community?
Free rides
Residents save money every time they use a bus or shuttle.
Faster service
Buses spend less time waiting for riders to pay fares.
Better access
People without cars have an easier time reaching work, school, appointments, and services.
Cleaner air
More public transportation use means fewer car trips, less fossil-fuel use, and less air pollution.
What's Happening Now?

No current public plan clearly calls for fare-free transit in Richmond. Several plans support better public transportation, safer walking and biking, and lower fossil-fuel use — these plans could provide a starting point for a free-transit program.

Who's Working On It?

A lead organization has not yet been identified. Possible partners include the City of Richmond, AC Transit, Richmond Moves, transportation advocates, and community organizations.

What's Still Needed?

Green Jobs & Industry

Conversion of Gas to Electric Cars

What Is the Project?

Local mechanics remove gas engines from existing cars and replace them with electric motors and batteries. A public subsidy would help low-income residents afford the conversion. The program would also train local mechanics, help shops purchase parts at lower prices, and connect residents with trusted conversion services.

Because the work must be done locally, it can support lasting jobs and small businesses in the region.

How Does It Help the Community?
Cleaner cars and air
Converting cars to electric reduces gasoline use and tailpipe pollution.
Lower driving costs
Electric cars often cost less to power and maintain than gas-powered cars.
Local jobs and businesses
Mechanics gain new skills and local shops can enter a growing clean-transportation industry.
Less waste
Converting existing cars reduces the need to build an entirely new vehicle and keeps useful cars on the road.
Access for lower-income residents
Public support helps residents who cannot afford a new electric car.
What's Happening Now?

The project is still in the idea stage and has not been directly included in a public plan. Several Richmond and regional plans support transportation electrification and clean-energy jobs. Electric car conversion businesses are growing in other places, and at least one online training course is available.

Who's Working On It?

A lead organization has not yet been identified. Possible partners include local mechanics, auto shops, workforce programs, clean-transportation groups, the City of Richmond, and regional funding agencies.

What's Still Needed?

A lead organization, a business plan, training partners, and funding for low-income residents. Partners also need to study safety rules, insurance, vehicle standards, parts supply, and the cost of each conversion.

How Can You Help?

Local mechanics can help shape the training and business model. Residents can also support funding for a pilot program that makes conversions affordable for lower-income drivers.
